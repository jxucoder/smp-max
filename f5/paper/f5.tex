\begin{abstract}
Let $f(n)$ denote the maximum number of stable matchings of a stable-marriage instance with $n$ men
and $n$ women. Determining $f(n)$ is a problem of Knuth (1976), restated as Open Problem~\#1 by
Gusfield and Irving (1989). Eilers reported $f(5)=16$ in 2022 (OEIS A357269) via a constraint-programming
search; the computation produced no proof object and the accompanying paper has not appeared. We give an
independent confirmation of $f(5)=16$ by a different method (a compact SAT encoding), and, to our
knowledge, the first machine-checkable certificate for the upper bound: an UNSAT proof of the statement
``some $5\times 5$ instance has at least $17$ stable matchings,'' emitted by \textsf{kissat} in DRAT
format (1.09\,GB) and verified by the independent checker \textsf{drat-trim}, which replayed
$10{,}398{,}816$ lemmas using $462{,}936{,}491$ resolution steps and emitted an LRAT certificate. Total
solver time was under ten minutes on a laptop. We outline the remaining steps toward a fully verified
proof in Lean~4, and discuss the first open case, the conjecture $f(6)=48$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A stable-marriage instance of order $n$ consists of $n$ men and $n$ women, each equipped with a strict
total preference order over the opposite side. A perfect matching $\mu$ is \emph{stable} if there is no
\emph{blocking pair}: a man $m$ and woman $w$, not matched to each other, who each prefer the other to
their partner in $\mu$. Gale and Shapley proved every instance has at least one stable matching. Let
$f(n)$ be the maximum, over all instances of order $n$, of the number of stable matchings. Determining
the behaviour of $f$ is Research Problem~\#5 in Knuth's 1976 monograph~\cite{knuth} and Open
Problem~\#1 of Gusfield and Irving~\cite{gi89}.

Asymptotically, the best bounds are $f(n) \ge 2.28^n$ (Thurber~\cite{thurber}) and $f(n) \le 3.55^n$
(Palmer and P\'alv\"olgyi~\cite{pp}, sharpening the first simply-exponential bound of Karlin, Oveis
Gharan and Weber~\cite{kow}). Exact values are recorded in OEIS~A357269~\cite{oeisA357269}:
\[
f(1),\dots,f(5) \;=\; 1,\; 2,\; 3,\; 10,\; 16.
\]
The values through $f(4)=10$ go back to Knuth's example and an exhaustive search of Eilers, which also
showed the extremal reduced instance of order~$4$ to be unique. The value $f(5)=16$ was announced by
Eilers in September 2022, obtained with a MiniZinc constraint-programming model; the OEIS entry cites a
paper ``in preparation'' which, as far as we can determine, has not appeared, and the computation
produced no independently checkable artifact---constraint solvers, unlike modern SAT solvers, emit no
proof of exhaustiveness.

This note does two things.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Independent confirmation.} We re-derive $f(5)=16$ by a different route: a direct SAT
encoding of ``there exists an instance of order $n$ with at least $k$ stable matchings.'' A solver
finds a witness with $16$ stable matchings in $0.1$ seconds and refutes $k=17$ in minutes. Two solvers
with unrelated codebases (\textsf{CaDiCaL} via PySAT, and \textsf{kissat}) agree on the refutation.
\item \textbf{A certificate.} The \textsf{kissat} refutation was run with proof logging, producing a
$1.09$\,GB DRAT proof, verified end-to-end by \textsf{drat-trim}~\cite{drattrim} in $530$ seconds. The
resulting LRAT certificate ($4.3$\,GB) is the format consumed by verified checkers, including those
available in Lean~4~\cite{lratcatcher}. To our knowledge this is the first proof object of any kind for
the upper bound $f(5)\le 16$.
\end{enumerate}

Comparable certified computations include the formal proof of the Ramsey number
$R(4,5)=25$~\cite{r45} and the Lean-certified evaluation of $K_8(4,2)=23$~\cite{k8}. We regard the
present note as the computational core of such a development for $f(5)$; the remaining formalization
steps are outlined in Section~\ref{sec:lean}.

\section{The encoding}\label{sec:encoding}

Fix $n$ and $k$. We construct a CNF formula $F_{n,k}$ that is satisfiable if and only if some instance
of order $n$ has at least $k$ stable matchings.

\paragraph{Preference variables.} For each man $m$ and each unordered pair $\{a,b\}$ of women, a
variable $p^m_{ab}$ meaning ``$m$ prefers $a$ to $b$''; symmetrically for each woman. A strict total
order is exactly a transitive tournament, and a tournament is transitive if and only if it contains no
directed $3$-cycle. So for every person and every unordered triple $\{a,b,c\}$ we add the two clauses
forbidding the two cyclic orientations. For $n=5$ this is $10$ variables and $20$ clauses per person.

\paragraph{Stability indicators.} For each of the $n!$ perfect matchings $\mu$, a variable $S_\mu$
with, for every non-matched pair $(m,w)$, the clause
\[
S_\mu \;\rightarrow\; \neg\bigl(p^m_{w\,\mu(m)} \wedge p^w_{m\,\mu(w)}\bigr),
\]
i.e., $S_\mu$ forces $(m,w)$ not to block $\mu$. Only this direction is needed:

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:enc}
$F_{n,k}$ is satisfiable iff some instance of order $n$ has $\ge k$ stable matchings.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
($\Leftarrow$) Given such an instance, set the preference variables accordingly and set $S_\mu$ true
exactly for $k$ of its stable matchings; every clause is satisfied. ($\Rightarrow$) A model's
preference variables define strict total orders (transitive tournaments), i.e., an instance; the
cardinality constraint (below) forces at least $k$ indicators true, and the implication clauses ensure
each such $\mu$ has no blocking pair, i.e., is genuinely stable.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Cardinality.} $\sum_\mu S_\mu \ge k$, encoded with a sequential counter.

\paragraph{Symmetry.} For the refutation run we add unit clauses fixing man~$1$'s preference list to
the identity order.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:sym}
If some instance of order $n$ has $\ge k$ stable matchings, then some instance in which man~$1$'s list
is the identity order has $\ge k$ stable matchings.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Relabel the women by the inverse of man~$1$'s preference permutation. Relabeling is a bijection on
instances that maps stable matchings to stable matchings bijectively.
\end{proof}

For $(n,k)=(5,17)$ with the symmetry clauses, $F_{5,17}$ has $1{,}971$ variables and $6{,}026$ clauses.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All runs on a single Apple M4~Max core (48\,GB RAM, macOS~26.3).

\paragraph{Sanity check at $n=4$.} The encoder reproves $f(4)=10$: $k=10$ is satisfiable (witness
recounted by an independent brute-force counter) and $k=11$ is refuted, both in $\le 0.1$ seconds. The
brute-force counter itself was validated against the extremal Latin instances of orders $3,4,5,6$
recorded in OEIS~A351413 (values $3, 10, 9, 48$, all reproduced).

\paragraph{Lower bound $f(5)\ge 16$.} \textsf{CaDiCaL}~1.9.5 finds a model of $F_{5,16}$ in $0.1$
seconds. Decoding yields the instance below (row $i$ = preference list of man/woman $i$, most
preferred first, participants numbered $1$--$5$); the independent counter confirms it has exactly $16$
stable matchings.
\[
\text{men:}\;
\begin{array}{l}
2\,3\,1\,5\,4\\
5\,1\,4\,2\,3\\
4\,5\,1\,3\,2\\
3\,2\,4\,5\,1\\
1\,5\,2\,3\,4
\end{array}
\qquad
\text{women:}\;
\begin{array}{l}
4\,1\,3\,2\,5\\
3\,2\,5\,4\,1\\
2\,3\,1\,5\,4\\
5\,1\,2\,3\,4\\
1\,4\,5\,3\,2
\end{array}
\]

\paragraph{Upper bound $f(5)\le 16$.} $F_{5,17}$ (with Lemma~\ref{lem:sym} clauses) is unsatisfiable.
Two independent refutations:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
solver & time & proof \\
\midrule
\textsf{CaDiCaL} 1.9.5 (PySAT) & $389.6$\,s & none (plain run) \\
\textsf{kissat} 4.0.4 & $\sim 8$\,min & binary DRAT, $1{,}093{,}088{,}497$ bytes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textsf{drat-trim} verified the DRAT proof by backward checking in $530.0$ seconds:
$10{,}398{,}816$ proof lemmas, of which $6{,}686{,}848$ in the trimmed core, checked with
$462{,}936{,}491$ resolution steps; $5{,}281$ of the $6{,}026$ input clauses are in the core; the core
contains $2{,}490$ RAT lemmas. The verdict was \texttt{s VERIFIED}, and an LRAT certificate of
$4{,}327{,}129{,}882$ bytes was emitted.

\begin{theorem}
$f(5)=16$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The instance displayed above has $16$ stable matchings (checkable by enumerating the $120$ perfect
matchings). For the upper bound, by Proposition~\ref{prop:enc} and Lemma~\ref{lem:sym} it suffices that
$F_{5,17}$ (with symmetry clauses) is unsatisfiable, which is witnessed by the verified DRAT/LRAT
certificate.
\end{proof}

\section{Toward a Lean-verified proof, and $f(6)$}\label{sec:lean}

Three components remain for a formalization in the style of~\cite{r45,k8}: (i) a Lean statement of
Proposition~\ref{prop:enc} connecting $F_{5,17}$, as a concrete CNF object, to a Lean definition of
stable matchings; (ii) Lemma~\ref{lem:sym}; (iii) importing the LRAT certificate. For (iii) we note
that the trimmed core contains $2{,}490$ RAT lemmas, while Mathlib's \texttt{lrat\_proof} implements
RUP-only checking; options include re-solving with RAT-introducing inprocessing disabled, converting
the proof, or using a RAT-capable verified checker~\cite{lratcatcher}.

The first open case is now $n=6$. The best known lower bound is $f(6)\ge 48$, attained by a Latin
instance built from the dihedral group (OEIS~A351413), and OEIS~A357271 records the conjecture that
$48$ is exact. The search space of reduced instances grows from ${\sim}10^{17}$ at $n=5$ to
${\sim}10^{28}$ at $n=6$, so a direct extension of the present method will require substantially
stronger symmetry breaking (only the $n!$ woman-relabeling symmetry is broken here; the men-relabeling
and side-swap symmetries remain), or a structural route through rotation posets, whose downset counts
equal the numbers of stable matchings.
